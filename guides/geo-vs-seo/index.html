GEO vs SEO

SEO earns a position in a list of links. GEO earns a presence inside the answer itself. Same goal — being found — on a different surface, with different evidence and different work.

What you optimize
A web page's position in a results list — in the GEO paper's terms, a website's impression in search is its average ranking over a range of queries.

The metrics
Average ranking and click-through rate; ranking stands in for visibility.
The paper: word count, position-adjusted word count, and subjective impression. Vendors: mention rate, citation rate, share of voice, sentiment, proprietary indexes. There is no unified caliber.
What content wins
Keyword stuffing is a classical SEO technique — listed in the paper as one of its baseline methods.
In the paper's experiments, keyword stuffing scored below the unoptimized baseline under GEO (position-adjusted word count 17.7 vs 19.3). Adding statistics, adding quotations, and citing sources were significantly positive — the best combinations gained 30–40%.
How stable results are
Rankings for the same query are relatively determinate, so an average ranking is a stable thing to track.
Generated answers are stochastic: the paper sampled 5 answers per query at temperature 0.7 and averaged 5 random seeds to control deviation. Google notes AI Mode and AI Overviews use different models and techniques, that the responses and links they show will vary, and that AI Overviews only appear when the system judges they add value — they often don't trigger. Measurement needs repeated sampling.
Technical requirements
Being indexed and eligible to be shown with a snippet is the entry condition.
The same. Google's official guidance: to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet — “There are no additional technical requirements.” Structured-data requirements are likewise the same as classic search.
llms.txt
Not applicable — classic search has no such file.
A community proposal, not a platform specification: llmstxt.org describes itself as a proposal for a curated overview file for agents, sitting alongside robots.txt and sitemap.xml. Google's AI features guide never mentions llms.txt, and no major engine we could check lists it as a requirement for appearing.
— Not either / or

Which one leads depends on where your buyers ask

This is a division of labor, not a contest. If your buyers still find you through search results pages — comparing vendors, reading reviews, clicking through — SEO is the main job and it is not finished: the entry conditions for AI surfaces are the same indexing and eligibility SEO already works on. If your buyers increasingly ask an AI assistant and act on the single answer it assembles, the answer itself is the surface, and that is GEO's job — measured in mentions and citations, not positions.

One honest counterweight, stated by the platform itself: Google's position is that GEO and AEO are terms you may see, but that optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience — and thus still SEO. On the technical layer that is simply true, as the table shows. Where the two part ways is the layer above it: what gets measured, and what work changes the number. New terms get exaggerated — one Forrester analyst has accused GEO's advocates of inflating the differences to carve out a new budget line — so judge by the rows, not the labels. (The definitions behind this page are on What is GEO.)

— FAQ

Straight answers.

Is SEO dead?

No — that is a magazine headline, not a fact. Google's own guidance is that appearing in its AI features requires exactly what classic search requires: an indexed page eligible to be shown with a snippet, with no additional technical requirements. SEO's foundations are the entry ticket to the AI surfaces, not a casualty of them.

–

Do I need both?

It depends where your buyers ask. If they search and click, SEO leads. If they ask an AI and act on its answer, GEO leads. Most small sites need SEO's foundation first — indexed, eligible, readable — and GEO's measurement once AI answers start sending, or withholding, their buyers.

+

Is llms.txt required?

No. llms.txt is a community proposal, not a platform specification — llmstxt.org calls it a proposal, and Google's AI features guide never mentions it. No major engine we could check lists it as a requirement for appearing in AI answers. It can still be a tidy overview file for agents; it is just not a ticket in.
