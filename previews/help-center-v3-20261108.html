How it works

The short version: we ask AI assistants the questions your buyers actually ask, and record whether your brand gets mentioned in the answers. Measured, not modeled.

  • Organic visibility only — whether AI assistants name your brand on their own in answers to real buying questions. Paid placements are never counted in your score.
  • Buyer-intent questions — “best X for Y” lists, comparisons, scenario advice. We deliberately don't pad scores with branded questions.
  • The engines — every plan runs the same three core engines: ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini. Growth adds Grok (4), Pro adds DeepSeek (5), Teams adds Mistral (6).
  • The rhythm — a full measurement runs weekly; between full runs, we watch your core questions daily and alert you to sudden moves.
  • What counts — visibility score = answers mentioning your brand ÷ valid answers × 100. Failed or timed-out runs don't count toward the score — they're reported separately as an error rate.
  • What you get — every week: your score change, what drove it, and 3 concrete actions — on-site, off-site, and technical.

We don't guarantee AI recommendations or rankings — no honest tool can. We measure credibly, explain plainly, and help you improve.

What CitMark measures

What does CitMark measure, and what does the score mean?

CitMark measures how visible your brand is in AI search: whether engines like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini mention you when people ask questions in your category, and what they say about you. Your visibility score summarizes that picture across the engines your plan covers, so you can track whether you're being mentioned more — and more accurately — over time. CitMark's job is to credibly measure, explain, and improve your visibility in AI search.

What is AI search visibility monitoring?

AI search visibility monitoring means checking, systematically and over time, how AI assistants answer the questions your buyers actually ask — and whether your brand appears in those answers. Instead of trying a question once yourself, you run a fixed set of category questions across engines like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini record every answer, and turn it into a visibility score: the share of answers that mention you. Because the same questions are re-run on a schedule, you can see your visibility move — and because every score is built from recorded answers, you can always check what it rests on.

What is the confidence interval next to my score?

Next to a CitMark score you'll see a range — for example, 50 (33–67). That range is a 95% confidence interval: the band where the score's true value most plausibly sits, given how many answers it was measured from. The width is information, not decoration. With few answers behind a score, the band is wide and the number is rough; as the sample grows, the band narrows and the number firms up. We always show the sample size next to the score, and any result based on fewer than 30 answers is flagged as low-sample — so you can tell at a glance how much weight a score can bear.

What is llms.txt?

llms.txt is a small file that tells AI what your site is about: a plain-text file at your site's root that lays out your key pages and what they cover, in a format AI systems can read easily. It's simple to add. One honest caveat: no AI provider has confirmed that llms.txt changes how they crawl or cite a site, so treat it as good housekeeping — not as a visibility lever by itself.

What kinds of questions does CitMark track for my brand?

Two kinds — and the difference matters more than the count.

  • Category questions are what buyers ask before they know your name: "best invoicing tool for a two-person agency", "air purifier for a small bedroom". These are the baseline of your score, because they show whether AI engines bring you up unprompted, in front of people who were never going to search for you directly.
  • Branded questions name your brand directly ("what does [your brand] do?"). They test whether engines describe you correctly — a different job.

CitMark never mixes the two into one number. Branded questions almost always look flattering — an engine that is asked about you by name will usually talk about you — so blending them in would inflate the score while hiding the category gaps where new buyers are won and lost. Each type is reported as its own layer, with its own sample size.

Every question in your set is versioned: if a question's wording changes, it becomes a new version, and results are never averaged across versions. That discipline is what makes period-to-period comparisons mean anything.

I asked ChatGPT the same question three times and got three different answers — none mentioned us. How can any tool claim to measure this?

You're describing the core difficulty of this category, not a flaw in your test: a single AI answer is one sample, and samples vary. Any single check — yours or a tool's — proves almost nothing on its own.

Measurement starts where single answers stop. CitMark asks a fixed, versioned set of questions repeatedly, across several AI engines, and reports your mention rate together with how many answers sit behind it and a confidence range. A brand that appears in 2 of 3 answers is a coin toss; a brand that appears in 40 of 120 answers is a measurement. When a layer doesn't have enough answers yet, we label it "insufficient sample" instead of dressing it up as a score.

So the honest claim is narrow: we can't tell you what ChatGPT will say next Tuesday at 9am. We can tell you, credibly and repeatably, how often engines mention you across many real buyer questions — and whether that's moving.

Improving your visibility

How does CitMark help me improve, not just measure?

Measuring is only half of it. Each CitMark report includes concrete next steps for the questions and pages where you're missing: what to change, and in what order. Then the next runs show whether your visibility actually moved — so a fix is something you can verify, not just hope for. One boundary we won't cross: AI engines decide for themselves what to mention and cite, so we never promise a ranking or a result — we promise a clear gap, a specific fix, and honest re-measurement.

Where do I start? How do I prioritize what to fix first?

Start where buyers actually are. Your score comes from category questions — the "best X for Y" questions people ask before they know your name — so the gaps that matter most are the category questions where your brand never appears while competitors do.

A practical order:

1. Find your absences. List the category questions with a mention rate of zero (or near it). Ignore your branded-question results for this — a brand people already search by name is not where growth is hiding.

2. Check the sample size first. If a layer is flagged "insufficient sample" (fewer than 30 valid answers), don't treat its number as a verdict yet — collect more runs before spending effort on it.

3. Follow the report's order for those questions. Each report's recommendations are already sequenced for the questions and pages where you're missing; work them in that order rather than picking at random.

4. Fix one batch, then re-measure. Change a coherent set of pages, let the next runs come in, and see whether the mention rate actually moved before starting the next batch.

One caution: a high score on branded questions can feel reassuring while category visibility — the part that brings new buyers — stays flat. Prioritize by category absence, not by which number looks worst.

I made changes — how do I tell whether they're working?

Judge changes the way the score is built — in layers, over repeated runs, never from a single answer.

  • Compare like with like. A before/after comparison only holds when the question set is the same version with the same mix. If the questions changed, you're comparing two different measurements.
  • Read the mention rate with its sample size and range. Every layer reports how many valid answers sit behind the rate, plus a 95% confidence range. If the sample is flagged insufficient (under 30 answers), the honest verdict is "not enough data yet" — not success, not failure.
  • Expect wobble. A single AI answer is an incomplete view; the same question can be answered differently run to run. That's why CitMark repeats runs across engines — judge the trend over several runs, not one screenshot.
  • Let the range narrow. As more runs accumulate, the confidence range tightens and real movement separates from noise. A change that worked shows up as a sustained shift in the layer it targeted — for example, category questions where you were absent starting to name you.

What we won't do is call a result early. If the data can't support a verdict yet, the report says so.

What kinds of changes help AI engines mention my brand?

AI engines mention brands they can find, understand, and quote. The changes that help are the ones that make your facts easy to do all three with:

  • State your facts plainly, in one place. What you offer, who it's for, where you operate, what it costs — written as clear statements on your own pages. Engines repeat facts they can lift cleanly; if your key facts are scattered, vague, or locked in images and scripts, there's nothing solid to repeat.
  • Keep the facts consistent. The same offer described three different ways across your site gives an engine three half-answers. One consistent version is easier to trust and to cite.
  • Be the source for your own category. Engines cite pages when answering. Pages that answer real buyer questions — comparisons, requirements, pricing logic — in plain language give engines something worth citing, and CitMark's reports show citations grouped by site, so you can see which of your pages (and which competitors' pages) engines actually draw from.
  • Give engines a map. An llms.txt file tells AI systems what your site is about and where the key pages are (see "What is llms.txt?"). It's a map, not a lever — it helps engines find the facts; the facts still have to be there.

Then measure: these changes work through mentions and citations over repeated runs, which is exactly what your reports track. No honest tool can promise an engine will mention you — anyone who guarantees that is selling certainty the engines themselves don't offer.

Why does the exact wording of a question change the result?

Because an AI engine doesn't answer the topic — it answers the sentence.

Short, list-shaped questions ("best CRM for freelancers") invite a list of brands. Long, situation-shaped questions ("I'm a freelancer drowning in spreadsheets, what should I do?") invite advice — often with no brand names at all. Questions carrying a budget or a deadline push the engine toward a decision; open-ended ones pull it toward an explanation. Same buyer, same need, four differently shaped questions — four genuinely different answers, and different chances for your brand to appear.

Two consequences follow, and CitMark's method is built around both:

1. A fixed, versioned question set. If the wording drifts, the measurement drifts with it — you'd be tracking the questions, not your visibility. Your tracked questions are locked and versioned so a score change means the answers changed, not the ruler.

2. No verdicts from a single answer. Even the identical question can draw a different answer on a different run. Scores come from repeated runs across engines, reported with their sample sizes — so the variation you're seeing is measured, not hidden.

If a result ever looks strange, this is the first place to look: check the exact wording and the sample size behind the number before concluding anything about your brand.

An AI engine is saying wrong things about my brand. What can I do?

First, separate two different problems — they have different fixes.

If the error is in the AI engine's answer: CitMark keeps the underlying answers behind your scores, together with the sources each answer cited — so you can open the exact answer that's wrong and see where it most likely picked the fact up. Engines assemble answers from the pages they draw on; a wrong price, a stale product name, or a service you no longer offer usually traces back to a page that still says it — yours, or a listing or profile about you.

The fix happens at the source:

1. Find the source. In your report, open the answer and check its cited sources for the wrong fact.

2. Correct it where it lives. Fix your own pages first (and keep the corrected fact consistent everywhere — see "What kinds of changes help AI engines mention my brand?"), then the third-party listings and profiles you control.

3. Re-measure. Engines pick up corrected sources over subsequent runs. There is no switch that edits an AI answer directly — we can't order an engine to change what it says, and neither can anyone else. What we can do is show you exactly what was said, where it came from, and whether it changes once the source is fixed.

If the error is in CitMark's own data — a mis-attributed answer, a citation attached to the wrong question — tell us. We verify it against the recorded run and correct it; measuring credibly is the whole product, so data errors are on us to fix.

I tried another brand-tracking tool and the data made no sense. Why would yours be any different?

Fair question — and the right standard. Data you can't check is data you shouldn't pay for. Three things make CitMark's numbers checkable rather than a matter of trust:

First, every score is built from answers you can open: the underlying AI answers and the sources they cited are kept behind the number, so a surprising result can be traced to what was actually said. Second, the method is published in plain terms — which questions, which engines, how the rate is calculated — including the discipline that branded questions ("what is [brand]?") are never blended into category questions ("best tool for X"), which is one of the easiest ways to manufacture a flattering score. Third, when the sample behind a number is too small to support it, the report says "insufficient sample" instead of printing false precision.

If a number still looks wrong, that's a support conversation we want to have — bring the question and we'll walk the underlying answers with you.

How long does CitMark keep my data?

How long we keep your data depends on the kind of data:

Full detail is in our Privacy Policy ("How long we keep it" and "Your rights: export and delete").

Where do the numbers in CitMark's data briefs come from?

Our published data briefs use anonymized, aggregated numbers only: every figure pools at least 10 unrelated sites over a window of at least one week, and no brief ever shows a single site's data. Your own numbers appear only in your own dashboard — never in a public brief. If we ever feature a named customer as a case study, that happens only with that customer's written consent, case by case.

Developers

I'm a developer — how do I work with CitMark data programmatically?

Programmatic access to CitMark data runs through MCP (read-only), included on every plan. Your own tools and AI assistants can read your scores, the underlying answers, and the citations behind them; the interface cannot change anything in your account.

The operating facts (the quota numbers themselves live in "Which plans include MCP access, and what are the read quotas?" — that entry is the source of truth):

  • Reads are counted per brand, and every API key belonging to a brand shares that brand's monthly quota and daily budget.
  • Answers come back as excerpts, and how far back reads can reach follows your plan's answer history.
  • Hitting the daily budget pauses reads until the next day; hitting the monthly quota pauses them until the next month. Treat a pause as a budget state, not an error.

General practice that works well with this shape (true of most quota'd read APIs, not CitMark-specific): pull on a schedule rather than continuously, cache responses on your side instead of re-reading the same window, and size each job to finish inside the daily budget so a pause never strands a half-done sync.

If you're evaluating whether the read surface covers your use case before building against it, contact support with what you're trying to pull — we'll confirm against the current quotas rather than have you guess.
